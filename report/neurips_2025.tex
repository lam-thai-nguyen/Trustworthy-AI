\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Machine-learning models can produce unequal outcomes across demographic groups
even when their average predictive performance is high
\citep{mehrabi2021survey}. A common response is to remove a sensitive
attribute, such as \texttt{sex}, from the model inputs. This intervention is
simple and often presented as a way to prevent discrimination. However, other
features may still contain information associated with the protected attribute
and allow the model to reproduce group disparities
\citep{datta2017proxy}. Removing \texttt{sex} therefore does not by itself
establish that a model is fair.

This paper studies that problem in income classification. We use the UCI Adult
Income dataset, which contains demographic, educational, occupational, and
work-related attributes and a binary target indicating whether annual income
exceeds \$50{,}000 \citep{dua2019uci}. The dataset provides a useful setting
for a controlled audit because it contains an explicit \texttt{sex} attribute
alongside several features that may encode social and economic structure.
Nevertheless, the dataset reflects a particular historical collection process,
uses a binary representation of sex, and cannot support universal conclusions
about fairness in income prediction. Our conclusions are therefore limited to
the observed groups, features, model choices, and audit design.

We ask whether excluding \texttt{sex} changes the fairness of an
income-classification model and whether remaining features can act as proxies
for it. To answer this question, we compare models trained with and without
\texttt{sex}, investigate whether selected remaining features contribute to
group-related disparities, and compare feature removal with fairness-aware
mitigation. Predictive performance and group fairness are evaluated together
so that an apparent fairness improvement is not interpreted without considering
its effect on utility.

An overview of our method is provided in Figure~\ref{fig:overview-proposed-method}. 
This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We provide a reproducible comparison of income-classification models
  trained with and without the explicit sensitive attribute \texttt{sex}.
  \item We combine model explanations, subgroup comparisons, and feature
  ablation to investigate whether specific remaining features behave as
  potential proxies.
  \item We compare proxy removal with fairness-constrained learning using
  common predictive and group-fairness metrics, making the associated
  performance--fairness trade-offs explicit.
\end{itemize}

The central contribution is not a claim that any one feature is inherently
unfair or that one fairness metric is sufficient. Rather, we provide an audit
of whether removing an explicit sensitive attribute is enough to change
observed disparities when related information remains available to the model.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Fairness through unawareness, proxy discrimination, and causal fairness.}
One influential response to discriminatory prediction is to omit protected
attributes from the model. However, \citet{dwork2012fairness} argue that
fairness cannot be reduced to ignoring sensitive attributes: their
\emph{Fairness Through Awareness} framework instead requires similar
individuals, under a task-specific similarity metric, to receive similar
treatment. This distinction is directly relevant to our comparison between
models with and without \texttt{sex}: removing the column is an unawareness
baseline, not evidence that the remaining representation is fair. The proxy
discrimination literature makes the same concern operational by studying how
protected-group information can be inferred from seemingly permissible
features and how such relationships can be audited \citep{datta2017proxy}.
By contrast, \citet{kusner2017counterfactual} define counterfactual fairness
through a causal model, requiring a prediction to remain unchanged in
counterfactual worlds in which an individual's protected attribute differs.
Our SHAP rankings, subgroup comparisons, and ablations are deliberately more
modest: they can identify predictive association and model dependence on
candidate proxies, but they cannot determine whether a feature is a causal
mediator, a legitimate correlate, or an impermissible proxy.

\paragraph{Group fairness criteria and the problem of incompatible objectives.}
A second body of work formalizes how disparities should be measured once a
model has been trained. \citet{hardt2016equality} introduce equality of
opportunity and equalized odds, connecting fairness to conditional error rates
and providing a post-processing procedure that uses group membership to alter
predictions. These ideas motivate our reporting of true- and false-positive
rate disparities in addition to selection-rate disparities. Yet no single
criterion resolves the broader normative question. \citet{chouldechova2017fair}
show that calibration and error-rate parity can conflict when outcome
prevalence differs between groups, while \citet{kleinberg2017inherent} prove
closely related impossibility results for risk scores. Critical reviews likewise
emphasize that criteria such as statistical parity encode different values and
can lead to different conclusions about the same system
\citep{corbett2017algorithmic,barocas2019fairness}. Accordingly, our audit
reports demographic-parity difference and equalized-odds difference as
separate descriptive outcomes alongside accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision,
recall, and F1. The Adult experiment therefore tests whether an intervention
changes particular measured disparities; it does not treat a small value for
one metric as a complete definition of fairness.

\paragraph{Explainability and feature-level auditing for proxy detection.}
The third relevant line of work uses explanations to inspect how models reach
their predictions. \citet{lundberg2017unified} introduce SHAP, an additive
feature-attribution framework grounded in Shapley values whose local accuracy
and consistency properties make it attractive for comparing contributions
across individual predictions. We use TreeSHAP in this spirit to rank features
in the sex-excluded XGBoost model and to prioritize variables such as
\texttt{relationship}, \texttt{marital-status}, \texttt{occupation}, and
\texttt{hours-per-week} for further investigation. SHAP, however, is an
explanatory device rather than a fairness test: a large attribution indicates
model reliance, not discriminatory intent or causal influence. This caveat is
especially important for Adult because its features are dependent.
\citet{aas2021explaining} show that common SHAP approximations can be
misleading when feature independence assumptions fail and propose
dependence-aware approximations and grouped explanations. Our use of
explanations is therefore paired with subgroup comparisons and feature
ablation. The combination asks a narrower and testable question than
explanation alone: whether removing a candidate feature changes both model
behavior and observed group disparities on the same held-out population.

\paragraph{Fairness-aware mitigation and constrained learning.}
Feature deletion is only one possible intervention, and it may remove useful
predictive information without directly controlling the disparity of interest.
\citet{zafar2017fairness} incorporate fairness constraints into the geometry of
a classifier's decision boundary, making the fairness--accuracy trade-off an
explicit part of training. \citet{madras2018learning} instead learn
representations that make sensitive attributes difficult to predict using an
adversary, relating the training objective to demographic parity, equal
opportunity, and equalized odds. The reductions approach of
\citet{agarwal2018reductions} is particularly close to our implementation:
it converts constrained binary classification into a sequence of
cost-sensitive classification problems and can optimize error subject to
demographic-parity or equalized-odds constraints. This provides the conceptual
basis for Fairlearn's \texttt{ExponentiatedGradient} comparison in our audit.
Our design places these approaches beside, rather than above, sex removal and
proxy ablation: all variants use the same fixed split and held-out metrics,
allowing us to distinguish fairness obtained by deleting features from fairness
obtained by explicitly constraining predictions. The comparison also preserves
\texttt{sex} for evaluation and mitigation constraints while excluding it from
ordinary predictive features, so that the fairness intervention is measurable
without treating the sensitive attribute as a standard model input.

Taken together, prior work establishes four points that motivate our study:
omitting a sensitive feature does not rule out proxy discrimination; fairness
criteria measure different and sometimes incompatible properties; explanations
can reveal model reliance but do not by themselves establish causality or
unfairness; and constrained learners offer a direct alternative to feature
deletion. The gap addressed here is an integrated, reproducible audit that
connects these pieces in one Adult-income experiment: it compares explicit
\texttt{sex} removal with targeted proxy ablation, uses SHAP and subgroup
diagnostics to justify which proxies are tested, and evaluates both feature
interventions and Fairlearn reductions under common predictive and group-fairness
metrics. This makes the practical claim more precise than either “remove the
sensitive attribute” or “optimize a fairness metric”: the question is which
intervention changes which observed disparities, at what performance and
explainability cost, under the stated dataset and audit limitations.


\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
\subsection{Our proposed method}
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-proposed-method} summarizes the proposed fairness
audit as a five-stage workflow: prepare the UCI Adult Income dataset, compare XGBoost
models with and without \texttt{sex}, identify and ablate candidate proxy
features, and apply fairness constraints before evaluating every variant on
the same held-out test set.

\paragraph{Prepare the data.}
We first clean and encode the dataset using a deterministic preprocessing
pipeline. Preprocessing is fitted on the training data only and then applied
to the held-out data to prevent leakage. The sensitive feature,
\texttt{sex}, is retained outside the model feature matrix so that it remains
available for subgroup auditing even when it is excluded from model training.
Further details about the dataset and preprocessing procedure are given in~\ref{subsec:Data}.

\paragraph{Compare models with and without \texttt{sex}.}
We train XGBoost models using the same data split, preprocessing, and decision
rule. One baseline includes \texttt{sex}, while a paired model removes only
that feature. This comparison isolates the effect of explicit feature removal
while leaving the model family and experimental conditions unchanged. The
sex-excluded model is therefore treated as an unawareness baseline, not as
evidence that the remaining features are free of sensitive information.

\paragraph{Identify candidate proxy features.}
We analyze the sex-excluded model with TreeSHAP and subgroup comparisons
\citep{lundberg2017unified}. Features are treated as candidate proxies when
they both contribute to predictions and show relevant differences across the
observed \texttt{sex} groups. This analysis focuses on features such as
\texttt{relationship}, \texttt{marital-status}, \texttt{occupation}, and
\texttt{hours-per-week}. These diagnostics identify model associations rather
than establish causal or normative claims about any feature.

\paragraph{Ablate proxies and retrain model variants.}
We test these candidate proxies through controlled ablation. Individual
features and selected feature sets are removed, and the XGBoost model is
retrained under the same conditions. Comparing the resulting variants with
the sex-excluded model shows whether proxy removal changes model performance,
group disparities, or feature-level explanations.

\paragraph{Apply fairness-constrained learning.}
We compare feature removal with Fairlearn's
\texttt{ExponentiatedGradient} reduction
\citep{agarwal2018reductions}. The constrained models use \texttt{sex} as the
constraint variable and impose either demographic-parity or equalized-odds
constraints, rather than using it as an ordinary model feature. This provides
a direct comparison between proxy-focused feature interventions and
fairness-constrained learning.

\paragraph{Common held-out evaluation.}
Finally, all model variants are evaluated on the same held-out data using a
common prediction rule. We compare predictive performance and group fairness
for the baseline, sex-excluded, proxy-ablated, and constrained models. The
specific evaluation measures and their definitions are described in~\ref{subsec:Metrics}.

\subsection{Dataset}\label{subsec:Data}
We use the UCI Adult Income dataset \citep{dua2019uci}, preserving its
official training and test partitions. The dataset contains demographic,
educational, occupational, and work-related variables, with a binary target
indicating whether annual income exceeds \$50{,}000. The sensitive attribute
\texttt{sex} is retained for subgroup evaluation even when it is excluded
from model features. The main descriptive
statistics are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-summary}. The table

eight categorical features, the imbalanced target distribution, the observed

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{subsec:Metrics}
We evaluate every model on the same held-out test split using predictive
performance and group fairness metrics. Let \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) denote the true
income label, \(\widehat{Y}\) the binary prediction, and
\(A=\texttt{sex}\) the sensitive attribute. The observed groups are indexed
by \(g\in\mathcal{G}\), and all metrics use the same fixed decision rule.

\paragraph{Predictive performance.}
We report accuracy (Acc), balanced accuracy (BAcc), precision (Pr), recall
(Rc), and F1. Accuracy is the fraction of all test records classified
correctly. Because the above-\$50{,}000 class is less frequent, we also report
balanced accuracy:
\[
  \mathrm{BAcc}
  = \frac{\mathrm{Sensitivity}+\mathrm{Specificity}}{2}
  = \frac{1}{2}\left[
    \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}
    + \frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}}
  \right],
\]
where sensitivity is the true-positive rate for the above-\$50{,}000 class
and specificity is the true-negative rate for the at-or-below-\$50{,}000
class. BAcc gives equal weight to the two income classes and is therefore
more informative than Acc when class frequencies are unequal. Precision is
the fraction of positive predictions that are correct, Rc is the fraction of
actual above-\$50{,}000 records identified, and F1 is their harmonic mean.
Together, these measures distinguish overall correctness from the model's
ability to identify the minority income class and the reliability of its
positive predictions.

\paragraph{Group fairness.}
We report demographic-parity difference (DPD) and equalized-odds difference
(EOD) across the observed \texttt{sex} groups. DPD compares positive
prediction rates without conditioning on the true income label:
\[
  \mathrm{DPD}
  = \max_{g\in\mathcal{G}} P(\widehat{Y}=1\mid A=g)
    - \min_{g\in\mathcal{G}} P(\widehat{Y}=1\mid A=g).
\]
This metric is relevant to the audit because it measures whether the model
assigns the positive income classification at different rates across groups,
which is the disparity that may remain after removing \texttt{sex} from the
features. It does not, however, account for differences in the observed
target distribution. With the difference defined over probabilities, DPD
has range \([0,1]\): \(0\) indicates identical positive prediction rates,
whereas larger values indicate greater disparity, with \(1\) being the
maximum possible difference.

EOD measures disparities conditional on the true label:
\[
  \mathrm{EOD}
  = \max\left\{
      \max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{TPR}_g
        - \min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{TPR}_g,\,
      \max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{FPR}_g
        - \min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{FPR}_g
    \right\},
\]
where \(\mathrm{TPR}_g=P(\widehat{Y}=1\mid Y=1,A=g)\) is the group-specific
true-positive rate and
\(\mathrm{FPR}_g=P(\widehat{Y}=1\mid Y=0,A=g)\) is the false-positive rate.
EOD is suitable here because it checks whether groups with the same observed
income label experience different opportunities for correct positive
classification or different rates of erroneous positive classification.
DPD and EOD therefore capture complementary notions: DPD measures disparity
in selection, whereas EOD measures disparity in conditional error outcomes.
Because both are differences of probabilities, EOD also has range
\([0,1]\). An EOD of \(0\) indicates identical TPR and FPR across the
observed groups, whereas larger values indicate greater disparity in at least
one of these rates, with \(1\) being the maximum possible difference. Thus,
lower DPD and EOD values indicate less observed group disparity, not
necessarily an unbiased or fair model. Zero is not a complete definition of
fairness, particularly given the dataset's binary \texttt{sex} representation
and the limitations of any single fairness criterion.
