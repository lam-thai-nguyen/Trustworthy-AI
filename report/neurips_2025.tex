\begin{abstract}
  Removing an explicitly protected attribute from a predictive model does not
  necessarily remove the information associated with that attribute. This
  report studies that issue in the UCI Adult Income dataset by comparing
  XGBoost models trained with and without the sensitive attribute \texttt{sex},
  investigating suspected proxy features with TreeSHAP and feature ablation,
  and evaluating a Fairlearn ExponentiatedGradient mitigation model. The
  analysis is intended to separate predictive performance from group-fairness
  measurements and to make the trade-offs introduced by feature removal and
  fairness constraints explicit. Empirical conclusions are limited to the
  dataset, preprocessing choices, model class, and fairness definitions used
  in this study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Machine-learning models are increasingly used to support decisions about
people, including decisions related to income, credit, employment, and access
to services. In these settings, evaluating predictive performance alone is
insufficient: a model can be accurate on average while exhibiting different
error rates or prediction rates across demographic groups. Fairness auditing
therefore requires both a clear definition of the protected groups and an
explicit comparison between predictive and group-level outcomes.

A common first intervention is to remove a sensitive attribute from the model
inputs. This intervention is appealing because it is simple and easy to
explain, but it does not guarantee that the resulting model is insensitive to
that attribute. Other variables may contain information correlated with the
protected attribute, either because they reflect social structure or because
they encode related aspects of an individual's circumstances. In this report,
we refer to such variables as potential \emph{proxy features}. The central
question is consequently not only whether \texttt{sex} is present in a model,
but also whether the remaining feature set allows the model to reconstruct
information associated with \texttt{sex}.

We investigate this question using the UCI Adult Income dataset, a standard
benchmark containing demographic, educational, occupational, and work-related
attributes with a binary target indicating whether annual income exceeds
\$50{,}000. The dataset is useful for a controlled audit because it contains
an explicit \texttt{sex} attribute together with several variables that may
encode related social and economic structure. At the same time, it is not a
neutral representation of the real world: its categories, missing-value
patterns, binary treatment of sex, and historical collection context limit the
claims that can be made from any experiment on it. Our conclusions are
therefore about this audit design and not about fairness in income prediction
in general.

The analysis follows a reproducible comparison of model variants. We first
train a baseline XGBoost classifier with \texttt{sex} available and compare it
with a model that excludes \texttt{sex}. We then examine
\texttt{relationship}, \texttt{marital-status}, \texttt{occupation}, and
\texttt{hours-per-week} as suspected proxies. TreeSHAP feature rankings,
subgroup comparisons, and feature-ablation experiments are used as
diagnostic tools for assessing whether these variables retain predictive
information associated with the sensitive attribute. These analyses are
exploratory: a high feature importance or a subgroup difference is evidence of
association in this dataset, not proof that a feature causes disparate
outcomes.

For each model, we report accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, and
F1 together with demographic-parity difference and equalized-odds difference
across \texttt{sex}. These metrics answer different questions and are not
interchangeable. In particular, reducing a statistical-parity difference can
come at the cost of predictive performance, while equalized odds focuses on
error-rate parity rather than parity of positive predictions. We therefore
keep predictive and fairness metrics in separate parts of the comparison
rather than treating one aggregate score as a complete definition of
fairness. We additionally evaluate Fairlearn's
ExponentiatedGradient procedure under a fairness constraint to examine the
trade-off between unconstrained prediction and constrained group outcomes.

This report makes three practical contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It provides a reproducible audit of whether excluding an explicit
  sensitive feature changes fairness measurements in an income-classification
  pipeline.
  \item It uses model explanations and ablation to test the narrower claim
  that particular remaining variables may act as proxies, rather than
  assuming that all correlated variables are interchangeable.
  \item It presents feature removal and algorithmic mitigation in a common
  comparison that distinguishes observed performance changes from the
  limitations of the selected fairness criteria.
\end{itemize}
The study does not attempt robustness analysis or threshold analysis, and it
does not treat statistical parity or equalized odds as sufficient conditions
for a fair decision system.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Fairness definitions and evaluation.}
Fairness research distinguishes individual-level requirements from
group-level statistical criteria. The ``fairness through awareness'' framework
of \citet{dwork2012fairness} requires similar individuals to receive similar
treatment under a task-specific metric, whereas our study evaluates group
differences across \texttt{sex}. For supervised classification,
\citet{hardt2016equality} formalized equality of opportunity and equalized
odds in terms of conditional prediction rates. These ideas directly motivate
our reporting of demographic-parity and equalized-odds differences alongside
accuracy and class-balanced performance. Our goal is not to select one metric
as a universal definition of fairness, but to show how conclusions about
feature removal and mitigation depend on which group-level property is
measured.

\paragraph{Proxy features and omitted sensitive attributes.}
The concern that a model can use protected-group information indirectly is
central to proxy-discrimination work. \citet{datta2017proxy} formalized
proxy non-discrimination as a property of data-driven systems in which
correlated attributes can influence outputs even when the protected attribute
is absent. We operationalize this concern in a narrower empirical setting:
we remove \texttt{sex}, rank candidate variables with TreeSHAP, compare
subgroups, and ablate \texttt{relationship}, \texttt{marital-status},
\texttt{occupation}, and \texttt{hours-per-week}. Thus, our contribution is
not a new formal definition of proxy discrimination; it is a reproducible
audit that connects proxy-focused diagnostics to predictive and fairness
outcomes in the same model family. The use of SHAP is diagnostic rather than
causal: an important or subgroup-associated feature is treated as a candidate
proxy, not as proof that the feature produces discrimination.

\paragraph{Mitigation and subgroup coverage.}
A second line of work changes the learning procedure instead of relying only
on feature deletion. \citet{agarwal2018reductions} reduced constrained fair
classification to cost-sensitive classification problems, providing the
algorithmic basis for the ExponentiatedGradient comparison in this report.
This lets us place two interventions in one audit: restricting the feature
set and imposing a fairness constraint while retaining the broader feature
representation. Finally, \citet{buolamwini2018gender} demonstrated that
aggregate performance can conceal substantial intersectional disparities.
Our primary audit is across the binary \texttt{sex} labels available in the
Adult dataset, so we do not claim to resolve intersectional fairness; instead,
we use this prior work to frame subgroup analysis and to make the dataset's
binary attribute representation an explicit limitation.


\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Our proposed method}
We propose a reproducible, diagnostic audit protocol for testing whether
removing an explicit sensitive attribute is sufficient to reduce group
disparities. The protocol treats feature deletion as one intervention in a
larger comparison rather than as evidence of fairness by itself. It has five
stages: (i) construct a leakage-controlled preprocessing and evaluation
pipeline, (ii) compare models with and without \texttt{sex}, (iii) identify
candidate proxy features using explanations and subgroup diagnostics, (iv)
measure the effect of ablating those candidates, and (v) compare feature-based
interventions with constrained learning. The sensitive attribute is retained
outside the model feature matrix throughout the experiment so that it can be
used for subgroup evaluation.

\paragraph{Leakage-controlled pipeline.}
The supplied Adult training and test files are cleaned with one deterministic
ETL procedure. Categorical variables are represented explicitly, including
the documented missing-value category, and the income label is mapped to a
binary target. Preprocessing components such as category encoding and numeric
transformations are fitted on the training split only and then applied to the
held-out test split. The target and \texttt{sex} are never used to fit
preprocessing for the model features. This separation prevents information
from the evaluation split from influencing training while preserving the
attribute needed for fairness measurement.

\paragraph{Model comparison.}
The primary predictive model is an XGBoost classifier trained under a fixed
configuration and evaluated with the same decision rule for every variant. We
first fit a baseline using the full permitted feature set, including
\texttt{sex}. We then fit a sex-excluded model after removing only
\texttt{sex} from the feature matrix. This paired comparison isolates the
effect of explicit attribute removal while holding the data split,
preprocessing, model family, and evaluation procedure constant. Additional
variants remove suspected proxies in a prespecified sequence, including
\texttt{relationship}, \texttt{marital-status}, \texttt{occupation}, and
\texttt{hours-per-week}. We report every attempted variant and do not
interpret a feature's removal as proof that the feature is intrinsically
unfair.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.}
The baseline uses the official Adult training file (32{,}561 records) for
fitting and the official Adult test file (16{,}281 records) for final
evaluation; no additional random split is drawn. The model input contains the
six numeric predictors and eight categorical predictors listed in the
dataset, including \texttt{sex}. Numeric columns are passed through and
categorical columns are one-hot encoded with unknown test categories ignored.
The transformer is fitted on the training split only. The resulting baseline
representation contains 108 transformed features. We use XGBoost with
300 trees, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.1, histogram tree construction,
and random seed 42; subsampling and column subsampling are both 1.0, and the
training objective is binary logistic classification with log-loss evaluation.
Predictions use the positive-class probability threshold 0.5. The saved test
predictions retain \texttt{sex} and the observed income label solely for
post-hoc evaluation.

\paragraph{Proxy diagnostics.}
Proxy analysis is performed only after fitting the sex-excluded model. We use
TreeSHAP to rank feature contributions to individual predictions
\citep{lundberg2017unified}, summarize the rankings over the evaluation
population, and compare feature distributions and prediction rates across the
observed \texttt{sex} groups. Features are carried forward as candidate
proxies when their model contribution and subgroup association provide
convergent evidence in this dataset. We then perform feature ablation by
retraining the model without one candidate or a prespecified candidate set.
The ablation comparison distinguishes three quantities: predictive change,
change in group metrics, and change in the explanatory profile. Because these
diagnostics are observational and model-dependent, they identify candidate
proxy pathways rather than establish causality.

\paragraph{Fairness-constrained comparison.}
As a complementary intervention, we train Fairlearn
ExponentiatedGradient models with the broader feature representation and
constraints based on demographic parity or equalized odds
\citep{agarwal2018reductions}. The sensitive attribute is supplied to the
mitigation procedure as a constraint variable, not as an ordinary predictive
feature. This comparison tests whether an explicit group constraint changes
the performance--fairness trade-off differently from deleting features. The
unconstrained baseline, sex-excluded model, proxy-ablation variants, and
constrained models are evaluated on the same held-out data and summarized in
one comparison table.

\paragraph{Decision and reporting rule.}
For each variant, we report accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall,
F1, demographic-parity difference, and equalized-odds difference. Lower
fairness-difference values indicate smaller observed disparities under the
corresponding definition, but no single metric is treated as a sufficient
fairness criterion. The protocol therefore reports predictive and fairness
results separately, records feature sets and preprocessing decisions, and
interprets changes only within the Adult dataset, selected model family,
binary \texttt{sex} representation, and fixed prediction rule.
\subsection{Dataset}
We use the UCI Adult Income dataset \citep{dua2019uci}, comprising 48{,}842
records and 14 predictors. The binary target is whether annual income exceeds
\$50{,}000, and \texttt{sex} is retained as a sensitive attribute for
evaluation. We preserve the dataset's supplied training and test files rather
than drawing a new split.
\paragraph{ETL}
The ETL implementation reads \texttt{adult.data} and \texttt{adult.test}
from \texttt{data/adult}. It removes surrounding whitespace, removes the
period appended to labels in the test file, validates the target labels and
numeric fields, and converts the target to 0/1. The documented \texttt{?}
values in categorical columns are mapped to an explicit \texttt{Missing}
category rather than being silently dropped. This affected 6{,}465 values:
2{,}799 in \texttt{workclass}, 2{,}809 in \texttt{occupation}, and 857 in
\texttt{native-country}. The cleaned training and test splits, combined
dataset, and ETL metadata are written to \texttt{data/processed}.
\paragraph{EDA}
The exploratory analysis is descriptive and is performed on the cleaned
combined dataset before model fitting. It reports descriptive statistics and
creates visualizations for the target distribution, age by income class,
income rates by education, numeric feature distributions, and documented
missing-value counts. We also compare the target rate across the two observed
\texttt{sex} categories as an initial descriptive fairness diagnostic; this
comparison is not a model fairness result. In the cleaned data, 11{,}687 of
48{,}842 records are above \$50{,}000. The corresponding observed rates are
30.38\% for males (9{,}918/32{,}650) and 10.93\% for females
(1{,}769/16{,}192).

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
We evaluate each model on the held-out test split using both predictive
performance and group fairness metrics. Predictive performance is summarized
by accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Accuracy is
the fraction of correct predictions; balanced accuracy is the mean recall
across the two income classes; precision is the fraction of predicted
above-\$50{,}000 cases that are correct; recall is the fraction of actual
above-\$50{,}000 cases that are identified; and F1 is the harmonic mean of
precision and recall. Reporting both accuracy and balanced accuracy is useful
because the target is not evenly distributed.

Fairness metrics are computed across the observed binary \texttt{sex}
categories, using the same fixed prediction rule for every model. The
demographic-parity difference is defined as
\[
  \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} P(\widehat{Y}=1 \mid A=g)
  - \min_{g \in \mathcal{G}} P(\widehat{Y}=1 \mid A=g),
\]
where \(A\) is \texttt{sex} and \(\mathcal{G}\) is the set of observed group
labels. It measures disparity in positive prediction rates, without
conditioning on the true income label. The equalized-odds difference is
reported as the larger of the across-group differences in true-positive rates
and false-positive rates:
\[
  \max\left\{
    \max_g \mathrm{TPR}_g-\min_g \mathrm{TPR}_g,\,
    \max_g \mathrm{FPR}_g-\min_g \mathrm{FPR}_g
  \right\}.
\]
Thus, it measures disparity in both types of conditional classification
outcomes. Lower values indicate smaller observed group differences for both
fairness measures, while zero does not establish that a system is fair in
all relevant senses.

The comparison table will include the baseline XGBoost model with
\texttt{sex}, a model with \texttt{sex} removed, models with suspected proxy
features ablated, and Fairlearn ExponentiatedGradient models constrained by
demographic parity or equalized odds. The same predictive and fairness
metrics will be reported for each variant so that feature removal, proxy
removal, and algorithmic mitigation can be compared without collapsing their
different objectives into a single score. We do not perform robustness or
threshold analyses; the reported values correspond to the prespecified
evaluation procedure and fixed prediction rule.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Data preparation and descriptive audit}
The ETL completed without dropping rows: all 48{,}842 records were retained,
including the documented missing categorical values. The cleaned data contain
37{,}155 records at or below \$50{,}000 and 11{,}687 records above \$50{,}000.
The descriptive target rate differs across the observed binary sex categories:
30.38\% among males (9{,}918/32{,}650) and 10.93\% among females
(1{,}769/16{,}192). This is a property of the dataset and is not a prediction
or a model fairness metric. It establishes the group labels and target
distribution used in the subsequent held-out evaluation.

\subsection{Baseline performance and fairness}
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-metrics} reports the first held-out baseline result.
On the 16{,}281-record test split, the model achieved 87.46\% accuracy and
79.90\% balanced accuracy. Precision was 77.86\%, recall was 65.57\%, and F1
was 71.19\%. These values describe the unconstrained model that includes
\texttt{sex}; they are not evidence that the model is fair or unfair without
reference to the explicitly reported group metrics.

The baseline positive prediction rate was 8.49\% for females and 25.59\% for
males, producing a demographic-parity difference of 0.1710. The true-positive
rates were 0.6102 and 0.6640, respectively, while the false-positive rates were
0.0207 and 0.0811. The equalized-odds difference was therefore 0.0604, driven
by the false-positive-rate gap rather than the true-positive-rate gap. These
results are descriptive of this fixed split, model, and threshold; they do not
establish causal discrimination or generalize beyond the dataset and audit
design.
